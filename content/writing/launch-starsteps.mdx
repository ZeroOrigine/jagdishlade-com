Every "free" learning app makes your child pay in some other currency. Data, attention, an upsell at exactly the moment they were finally focused.

I want to talk to one specific parent right now. You're the one who hands over the tablet at 7pm because it's the only twenty quiet minutes of your day, and then you watch, out of the corner of your eye, as an ad for something loud interrupts the fractions lesson. Or you get the popup asking to "create a profile" before your seven-year-old can even see the next question. Or you're on a train, or at a relative's house with no wifi, and the app just sits there, spinning, useless.

## The kitchen table problem

Here's the scene I'm picturing. It's a weekday evening. Homework is done, or half done. Your kid wants "the game with the stars" for a bit before bed. You want that too, honestly, because it buys you time to make dinner or answer one more email. But every app in that category has a catch built into its business model. It needs an account to build a profile on your child. It needs ad impressions to make money. It needs you to hit a paywall around lesson six so the "free" trial converts.

None of that is your kid's fault. They just want to climb from one star to the next. The friction is on your side, the parent who has to vet, sign up, decline the marketing emails, and hope the data stays where it's supposed to.

## What reconciliation taught me about trust

I spent years as a Chartered Accountant before I started building autonomous systems. In accounting, a number you cannot reconcile is a number you cannot trust, no matter how good it looks on the surface. You trace it back to its source. If the trail disappears into a black box, that's not a small issue, that's the whole issue.

I think about apps for kids the same way. If a "free" product needs your child's data to survive, the ledger doesn't balance. Somewhere, someone is monetizing the very time your child spends learning. It might be invisible to you. It is never invisible to the business model. And a business model you can't see is a number that doesn't reconcile.

That's the dot I keep connecting between spreadsheets and software: trust isn't a feeling, it's a structure. Either the incentives line up with the child's interest, or they don't. Ads and accounts and cloud profiles are usually a sign they don't.

## Back to zero, on purpose

My whole approach to building things is to remove steps, not add features. Star Steps came out of asking: what is the absolute minimum a kid needs to learn something new, and what is everything else that's just been bolted on for someone else's benefit?

The answer, for us, was 810 skills across 16 subjects, from Kindergarten through Grade 6, delivered as a game. No sign-up screen. No ad break. No cloud dependency. It works offline, so it doesn't care if you're on a train or in a basement with no signal. Progress stays on the device, because a five-year-old's arithmetic scores don't need to live on a server somewhere waiting to be a data point in someone else's dashboard.

Every lesson is free. Not free-with-an-upgrade-path. Free, full stop. That was the design decision that mattered most, because it's the one that removes the step where a parent has to decide whether their kid's learning is worth a monthly charge, or worse, worth their data.
