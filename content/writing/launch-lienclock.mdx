You did the work. The GC signed off on it. And you may have already lost your legal right to get paid for it, weeks before the invoice was even due.

That's not a threat. That's how lien law works in most states, and almost nobody in the trades finds out until the money is stuck.

## The clock starts before you notice it running

Picture the sub who frames three houses a month. He shows up, does clean work, invoices on time. He is good at his trade. He is not a lawyer, and nobody ever told him he was supposed to be one.

In most states, your right to file a mechanic's lien if you don't get paid depends on a notice you were supposed to send near the start of the job, sometimes within 20 days of first furnishing labor or materials. Miss that window and it doesn't matter how good your work was, or how clearly you were shorted. The right to lien just isn't there anymore. It expired quietly, on a date nobody put on your calendar, in a rule that's different in every state and different again depending on whether the GC is public or private.

Nobody sends you a reminder for this. The GC certainly won't, because a preliminary notice protects you, not them. Your invoicing software doesn't know construction lien law. Your bank doesn't care. The only person whose job it was to track that date was you, while you were also framing houses.

That's the actual problem. Not "I don't get paid sometimes." It's "I have a real legal right to protect myself, and I am losing it by accident, over and over, because tracking deadlines was never part of the job I trained for."

## What a CA learns about clocks

I spent years reconciling ledgers before I built anything with a screen. Reconciliation teaches you one thing above all else: a right you don't exercise in time stops being a right. It becomes a write-off.

Statutes of limitations work this way. Warranty claims work this way. Insurance claims work this way. And lien rights work this way, except the window is shorter and the stakes for a small sub are bigger, because that unpaid invoice might be the difference between making payroll and not.

The pattern is always the same. Somewhere there's a real, enforceable right. And somewhere there's a date, buried in a statute nobody in the trade reads, that quietly cancels it. The right was never the problem. The tracking was.

I think about innovation the same way I think about a ledger that won't balance: the fix is rarely a new feature. It's removing a step that shouldn't have needed a human in the first place. Back to zero. What's the smallest, dumbest thing standing between you and the outcome you already deserve? Usually it's a date nobody wrote down.

## The step LienClock removes

LienClock removes exactly that step: knowing and tracking the deadline.

You enter three things: the job's state, the GC, and the start and completion dates. That's it. LienClock calculates every preliminary notice deadline, every lien filing deadline, and every enforcement deadline that applies to that job, because it knows the rules differ by state and applies the right ones to your dates. Then it sends you email and SMS reminders so the deadline reaches you before it passes, not after.

It does not file anything for you. It's not a lawyer and it doesn't pretend to be one. What it does is make sure you, the person who actually has the right to be paid, never lose that right because you were busy doing the job the deadline was protecting in the first place.

That's the whole idea. Not automating your legal filing. Just making sure the clock rings before it runs out.
