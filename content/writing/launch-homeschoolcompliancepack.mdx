## The fear isn't the curriculum

You can teach your kid fractions. You can figure out phonics on a Tuesday afternoon with a whiteboard and a cup of coffee. What you can't figure out, not easily, is whether your state wants a notarized notice of intent by August 15th or September 1st, whether you need to test in the spring or just keep "records," and what that word "portfolio review" actually means in your jurisdiction versus the one your homeschool group friend lives in three counties over.

That's the real problem. Not teaching. Compliance.

I hear from parents who've built beautiful, thoughtful homeschools and still lie awake wondering if they missed a form. One overlooked date and you're not dealing with a lesson plan anymore, you're dealing with a school district letter, or worse. The stakes of getting the paperwork wrong are higher than the stakes of a bad math day. Nobody warns you about that when you decide to homeschool. You research reading programs. You don't research administrative law.

## The dot I keep connecting

I used to be a Chartered Accountant. My whole professional life was built around one idea: the number either reconciles or it doesn't. There's no partial credit on a balance sheet. A deadline missed by a day is the same, legally, as a deadline missed by a year.

Homeschool compliance runs on the exact same logic, except most parents don't have an accountant's brain wired for it. They have a teacher's brain, a parent's brain, a brain built for patience and repetition and figuring out why a kid keeps reversing his b's and d's. That brain is not built to track fifty different state statutes, each with its own testing windows, its own notice deadlines, its own required forms.

This is the same failure mode I see everywhere I look: important, high-stakes tracking gets left to memory, or to a sticky note, or to "I'll google it again closer to the date." The gap between "I know I need to do this" and "I actually did it, correctly, on time" is where good, careful people get penalized for administrative reasons that have nothing to do with whether they're doing right by their kids.

## What the tool actually removes

You pick your state. That's it, that's the input.

From that, you get a pre-filled notice-of-intent template, already formatted for what your state specifically requires, not a generic template you have to interpret and adapt. You get a personal calendar built from your state's actual filing and testing dates for the year, not a list of every state's rules that you then have to filter yourself. And you get an email reminder two weeks before each date, so the deadline finds you instead of you having to remember to go find it.

The step that disappears is the research step. Not the compliance itself, that's still real, still yours to do, still legally required. What disappears is the hour, or the many hours, spent digging through state education department websites written in bureaucratic language, cross-referencing forums, trying to figure out if the form you found is current or three years out of date.

I don't build features. I remove steps. This one removes the step where you become, temporarily, an expert in state homeschool law, just so you can file one form correctly.
