A lapsed license doesn't announce itself. It doesn't send a warning flare. It just quietly stops being valid, on a specific date, in a specific state, while you keep seeing clients, signing off on work, billing hours, as if nothing changed. You find out weeks later, usually from a board letter, sometimes from a client who checked.

## The spreadsheet that lies to you

I know a version of this problem well, because I spent years in the world of reconciliation. Books either match reality or they don't. There's no partial credit. A ledger that's "mostly right" is wrong.

Licensing works the same way, except most people don't treat it that way. If you hold a license in more than one state, or you're the kind of professional whose board requires a set number of CE hours every one, two, or three years, you're running a small reconciliation exercise in your head. State A wants 24 hours by June. State B wants 30 hours, split between ethics and core content, by a different date entirely. You took a course in March, another in September, half a dozen certificates sitting in a downloads folder or a Gmail label named "CE - do not delete."

Nobody sits down monthly and checks whether your actual hours match what each state actually requires. You do it in a rush, usually a few weeks before a deadline you half-remember, hoping the math works out. Sometimes it doesn't. Sometimes you find out a course didn't count toward the category you needed. Sometimes you find out too late to fix it.

## The wider pattern: rules and reality drift apart when nobody's watching

Here's the dot I keep connecting, across every field I've touched, from ledgers to logistics to licensing: two things that are supposed to match, rarely do, once time and distance get involved. The rule stays written down somewhere. Your actual activity happens somewhere else. Nobody's job is to sit between the two and keep whispering "still matching, still matching, still matching." So they drift. Quietly, gradually, until the gap is big enough to cost you something.

Accountants have known this for centuries, which is why reconciliation is a discipline, not a task. You don't reconcile once a year and hope. You reconcile continuously, in small steps, so the gap never gets a chance to grow into a crisis.

Licensing boards never gave professionals that tool. They gave you a rulebook and a due date, and left the tracking to you, manually, across however many states you happen to practice in.

## What RenewalRadar CE actually removes

RenewalRadar CE isn't a course provider. It doesn't care where you get your CE hours, which is the point. It holds two things side by side, permanently: the state rules for your license, and your actual logged hours against them. Then it counts down, per license, so you're not the one doing the mental math every few months trying to remember whether Nevada wants 20 or 24, and whether you're at 14 or 18.

The step it removes is the reconciliation itself. Not the courses. Not the studying. Just the quiet, error-prone job of holding two changing numbers in your head and hoping they still line up. You log what you've done. It knows what's required. It tells you the gap, if there is one, early enough to close it.

That's the whole idea. Back to zero: not more dashboards, not another portal to check, just the one thing that actually needs watching, watched.
